Convergence

Signal Convergence Scoring

Individual indicators are noisy. SignalForge counts how many agree and computes a convergence score — the percentage of indicators voting in the same direction. This feeds into the 5-component composite score that drives the unified BUY/HOLD/SELL signal.

// 7 of 10 indicators have definitive data, 5 vote BUY
convergence = (5 / 7) × 100 = 71.4 → strong buy agreement
Data source: All price data is fetched via Yahoo Finance v8 chart API with 6 months of daily candles. Extended-hours prices use the 1-day/2-minute intraday endpoint with pre/post market data included.

📈 IV / HV Analysis

Implied vs. historical volatility — time your entries with precision

Volatility Charts

IV vs. HV Comparison

Dual-line chart showing implied volatility (from option prices) versus historical/realized volatility (from actual stock price movement) over time. When IV is significantly above HV, options are "expensive" — good for sellers. When IV is below HV, options are "cheap" — good for buyers.

// Historical Volatility (annualized)
HV = σ(daily log returns) × √252

// Implied Volatility
IV = derived from Black-Scholes model via ATM option prices

// IV Percentile (where current IV sits vs. past year)
IV_Rank = (currentIV - 52wkLow) / (52wkHigh - 52wkLow) × 100
Trends

IV Trend Analysis

Track how implied volatility trends over time to identify vol expansion/contraction cycles. Rising IV before earnings? Expect a vol crush after the announcement. Falling IV in a quiet market? Premium sellers get squeezed. The trend lines help you time when to buy vs. sell options.

IV StateInterpretationStrategy Implication
IV < HVOptions are cheap relative to actual movesBuy options (long calls/puts)
IV > HVOptions are expensive, market pricing in more riskSell options (covered calls, CSPs)
IV ≈ HVOptions fairly priced — no vol edgeFocus on directional signals instead

🎯 Probability Engine

Statistical distributions showing where the market expects price to land

Model 1

Lognormal Distribution

The classical model assumes stock returns are normally distributed (prices are lognormally distributed). Given the current price, implied volatility, and time to expiration, it computes the probability density for every possible future price.

// Lognormal probability density function
μ = ln(S₀) + (r - σ²/2) × T
σ_T = σ × √T
P(S) = (1 / (S × σ_T × √(2π))) × exp(-(ln(S) - μ)² / (2 × σ_T²))
Visual output: A bell-curve-shaped distribution plotted across price levels. The peak shows the most likely price at expiration. The width reflects implied volatility — higher IV means a wider, flatter curve.
Model 2

Breeden-Litzenberger Implied Distribution

Unlike the lognormal model (which assumes symmetry), the Breeden-Litzenberger method extracts the market's actual probability distribution from observed option prices. It captures skew — the market's real fear of crashes or euphoria about rallies.

// Risk-neutral density from option prices
f(K) = e^(rT) × (∂²C / ∂K²)

// In practice: finite differences across strikes
f(Kᵢ) ≈ e^(rT) × (C(Kᵢ₊₁) - 2C(Kᵢ) + C(Kᵢ₋₁)) / (ΔK²)

This produces an asymmetric distribution that reflects the actual skew priced into the option market — often showing a "fat left tail" (crash fear) that the lognormal model misses.

Threshold impact: Tighter thresholds (55/45) → more trades, more signals, more noise. Wider thresholds (75/25) → fewer trades, higher conviction, more missed moves. The default 65/35 gives a 30-point Hold zone, which is a moderate stance.
